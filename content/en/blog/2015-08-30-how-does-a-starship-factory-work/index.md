A catalogue of questions about Starship Factory reached us by email, which could be very
interesting for readers of this blog too. So we are publishing our answer here:

### What infrastructure is on offer and how was it financed?

Under [Equipment in our wiki](https://wiki.starship-factory.ch/Equipment/) there is a list of
some of the equipment in our space. Unfortunately we still have a publication deficit in that
respect. Most of the equipment is privately owned by members who make it available to others,
but the association owns a few machines as well (the
[3D printers](https://wiki.starship-factory.ch/Equipment/3D-Drucker/), the
[soldering stations](https://wiki.starship-factory.ch/Equipment/Loetstation/) or the
[visitor laptops](https://wiki.starship-factory.ch/Equipment/IT/Besucherlaptops/), for
instance).

### How is the day-to-day running of Starship Factory organised?

We pay our rent and have two members who voluntarily make sure (by being there or by
delegating) that someone is always present during our official opening hours on Tuesday and
Friday. In addition some members have a key and therefore permanent access. It will soon be
made visible on the website when the space is open outside opening hours as well.

The more important decisions about the space, which cannot be settled within a do-ocracy
(whoever does it is right) because, say, the association's finances are affected, are taken at
the monthly plenary. The members who are interested in the topics on the table turn up there.
That is often very few, but the institution of the plenary is very helpful, because members —
even when they do not turn up to the plenary — find it easier to accept the decisions taken
there, since they could have influenced them.

### How is Starship Factory financed (ongoing income and expenditure)?

As you can see from [the financial plan for 2015](https://wiki.starship-factory.ch/Vereinskram/Finanzplanung/2015/),
we finance ourselves roughly 90% from membership fees and 10% from workshops and other income.
Our running costs are listed under
[the heading financial planning](https://wiki.starship-factory.ch/Vereinskram/Finanzplanung/).

Machines are, as already mentioned, mostly financed by members. That is the only way to manage
the frankly steep start-up investments needed to make it possible in the first place to carry
out more complex, cross-disciplinary projects in the space (designing, etching and populating
circuit boards, then building a case out of wood or plastic and/or sewing it into clothing, for
example). It is extremely important to find sensible purchasing routes — clever buying can often
save up to 95% of the purchase price and so build up a kind of stock.

### Which themes are important to Starship Factory, and why are those the focus?

We take a lot of our bearings from the
[Hackerspace Design Patterns](https://wiki.hackerspaces.org/Design_Patterns) and from
recommendations out of the field of [geek feminism](http://geekfeminism.wikia.com/).

Our most important theme is community building. We are convinced that it is very important (and
unfortunately also very difficult) to build a community in such a way that it is genuinely open
to everyone and that everyone can feel welcome and comfortable in it. But we also believe that
such a community is the only route to a space's long-term success. What matters is setting
sufficiently strict rules of conduct with plenty of good judgement, and enforcing them — not
tolerating it when someone drops a remark that makes other members no longer feel welcome. That
also requires appropriate training of the members; we are still working on that. But there is a
lot of training material from the fields of feminism and stereotype bias and similar areas.

It is also important not to concentrate solely on the productive aspect of the space, but on the
aesthetic aspects and the opportunities to relax as well. A hackerspace is more than just a
production environment: it is a living space, which should feel inviting and comfortable, since
that is the environment in which ideas come about. To that end we have a relaxation corner with
floor cushions and are working on attractive, intelligent lighting.

The second most important theme, in our view, is the availability of information. It is
important at every level to have a "transparent hackerspace". For people who have never been
there, it has to be clear:

1. Where is the space, and what happens there? What criteria do I have to meet to be welcome?
2. Is the space open right now? When will it next be open?
3. How does the community in the space work? How should I behave?
4. Which machines am I allowed to use? What do I need an introduction for? Where do I get it?
5. What do I actually do with this machine?
6. What does it cost if I do X? How does the price come about?
7. How did the space come to be the way it is? How can I get involved? What happens to my
   money?

Everything in the space has to be transparent and comprehensible, as long as it does not intrude
on members' privacy. That is the only limit that should be accepted; anything else is nonsense.
Published finances, for instance, bring a small potential disadvantage when looking for a space,
but a big advantage with members and applicants, who feel confident that their money is well
invested and that they can get involved if they want to. It is also important that it is made as
easy as possible for anyone to walk into Starship Factory and make something. Labels and QR
codes are very helpful for that, but the documentation that goes with them has of course to be
complete, correct and up to date.

If you observe those two points, you get a great hackerspace with an enthusiastic community.
Projects then mostly come about all by themselves, machines arrive, and the space works. That is
also why the answer to this question does not fall into the typical pattern of "what sort of
projects do you do?"; the task of a hackerspace is not to provide project ideas, but to offer
material, equipment and information so that visitors can put their own thoughts into practice.
