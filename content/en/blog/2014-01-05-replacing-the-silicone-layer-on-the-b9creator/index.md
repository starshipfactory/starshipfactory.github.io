When 3D printing with the B9Creator, UV resin is cured layer by layer with the help of a
projector and so turned into a shape, which is lifted out of the remaining still-liquid resin
after the print. So that the resin does not bond with the acrylic glass print tray (called the
vat in the manuals) as it cures, a coating of silicone (PDMS) is used. Unfortunately this
breaks down after a few prints and therefore has to be renewed regularly.

We settled on silicone of the brand
[Wacker Elastosil 601](http://www.wacker.com/cms/de/products/product/product.jsp?product=10461)
with Shore hardness 45. Unlike the [Sylgard 184](http://www.dowcorning.com/applications/search/default.aspx?r=131en)
used originally, it is available locally and even has slightly better adhesion to the acrylic
glass of the print tray. We shall see, and report, how it behaves in terms of durability
during 3D prints.

To repair the PDMS coating successfully with the Elastosil, it is best to use a levelling
table. If you do not have one to hand, the do-it-yourself variant works excellently: put a
spirit level on a plate with three levelling screws and any unevenness is quickly compensated
for.

For mixing the silicone a letter scale is recommended, since you have to mix two components of
45 g and 5 g respectively. A scale accurate to 1 g may produce an error of 10% at a quantity
of 5 g, since you never learn whether there are 4.5 g or 5.4 g in the cup. Letter scales show
tenths of a gram and therefore do not have this problem. With letter scales and measuring cups
such a mixing ratio should be easy to achieve.

Since UV resin printing is an optical process, in which the object is irradiated with UV light
from below, it is important that the silicone contains as few air bubbles as possible, since
they can deflect and disturb the light rays. The Elastosil we use has a high viscosity and
therefore does not really need to be degassed separately, provided the mixing ratio above is
accurate enough. Any bubbles rise on their own and burst at the surface.

If you heat the vat to a maximum of 70 °C to cure the silicone, it can be used again after
just 3–4 hours. That does however increase the risk that not all the air bubbles in the
silicone reach the surface before it has finished curing. Without heat, curing the silicone
takes up to 48 hours, which does make the silicone more likely to be free of bubbles. It is
therefore worth working with a second vat, so the B9Creator can be kept in operation.

Because of mistakenly different Z-axis settings in the slicer and the printing program, it came
out stretched. Astonishingly, the print result is still presentable. 100 microns of the
original object correspond to 88.5 microns in the print shown above.
